FaultLines

A visual-analytics tool for the place where asking several language models stops working: when they all agree, and all of them are wrong.

the published idea

“If the models disagree, one of them is probably wrong.”

So ask several models the same question. Measure how much they differ. Use that as a warning light for hallucination.

It works, and it is already published. Farquhar et al. (Nature, 2024) report a detection score of AUROC 0.790? for one model disagreeing with itself; Gorbett & Jana (2026) report AUROC 0.727 for the cross-model version, both on other datasets and so not directly comparable. This project reproduces the same measure at AUROC 0.840 on 34,062 questions — so the signal is not the contribution.

the gap disagreement detectors do not measure

What if they all agree — and they are all wrong?

Disagreement can only warn you about errors that show up as a disagreement. A shared mistake produces perfect consensus and a confident, quiet, wrong answer.

Every detector built on disagreement is blind here — by construction, not by accident. This project calls these cases silent consensus (blind spots) and builds the view that finds them.

the premise, measured

Four developers is supposed to mean four independent brains. It does not.

Within each dataset, multiply the four models' error rates to get how often independence says all four should be wrong at once, and add the datasets up. The four are wrong together 3.3× as often as that (95% CI 3.24–3.41).

Within each dataset the mean pairwise error correlation is φ = 0.44–0.60, where independence would be 0. One plausible reason, not tested here: they learned from much of the same web.

The third bar is the thesis. Wrong together is survivable — four models can be wrong four different ways, and then they disagree and the detector fires. Wrong and unanimous is the case the warning light cannot see.

Pooled over the whole corpus the figure is larger — all four wrong on 31.8% against 5.2% predicted, 6.1×, φ 0.654 — and it is the weaker number: it multiplies error rates averaged over datasets whose difficulty differs several-fold, and that alone manufactures co-failure. the questions themselves

Four benchmarks, picked for how hard they are

PopQA13,760 questions · 55% wrong?

Wikipedia entities running from household names down to almost nobody, each tagged with how many people actually view its page. The obscurity end.

HaluEval10,000 questions · 50% wrong?

Built to catch hallucination: every question ships the true answer and a convincing fake. The richest source of blind spots we measured, by rate.

TriviaQA9,962 questions · 14% wrong?

Ordinary trivia, asked with no reference text. The easy end — and that is its job: a detector score needs questions the models get right too.

TruthfulQA340 questions · 17% wrong?

Questions where the popular answer is the false one. Too small to carry statistics — kept for its human-written list of why each item fools people.

The ensemble gets 14% of TriviaQA wrong and 55% of PopQA. That spread is the whole reason for using four: a warning light can only be scored on a corpus that contains both errors to catch and correct answers to leave alone, and no single benchmark supplies both. The detection signal and the error correlation hold in all four.

950 questions — 2.8% of the corpus, and 8.8% of the questions every model got wrong.

Nothing is thrown away here. All 34,062 questions stay in the corpus, in the map and in the detection score — this is a definition, not a filter. Read it the other way and it says the same thing: the four models agreed on 12,181 questions?, and on 7.8% of those every model was wrong.

"So just make it say I don't know when confidence is low."? Measured, not argued. In this region a threshold that catches 80% of the errors also destroys 77% of the correct answers — confidence averages 0.947 when right and 0.929 when wrong, and no line fits inside that.

These are the grader's counts. About a fifth (21.1%) turn out to be answer-key errors, not model errors, which leaves 750 real shared mistakes (slide 18).

So this is something the ensemble does, not something one nasty test set does to it — which means the tool is for any corpus, not just adversarial ones.

This is the safety argument: in the one upgrade measured here, a larger share of the remaining mistakes is the kind a disagreement check cannot see.

Why is the error rate so high? Because these benchmarks are chosen to be hard — obscure Wikipedia entities and questions written to bait a wrong answer. Nothing is filtered out first; these are raw rates over all 34,062 questions. A warning light can only be measured where there are errors to warn about.

result 3b — what the blind region is made of

It tracks how correlated the models are. Not how good they are.

Eight models have answered the identical corpus, so every subset of two, three or four of them is an ensemble that could have been deployed. 154 of them, each fully re-clustered by meaning, no new generations, nothing spent.

ensemble sizeblind-spot raterange
4 models — the headline 2.79% —
3 models 4.01% 3.86–4.18%
2 models 7.51% 7.13–8.84%

2025 models only, so no generation is mixed in. Never quote the headline rate without this curve — dropping one model of four raises the blind region by 44%.

within each sizek=2k=3k=4
error correlation → blind rate +0.87+0.91+0.92
error rate → blind rate -0.03-0.22-0.34

You cannot buy your way out with better models. A less correlated ensemble has a smaller blind region, and correlation is measurable before you deploy.

And the trade is not free, which is the opposite of what I predicted: correlation also tracks detection AUC (k=2 +0.93 · k=3 +0.73 · k=4 +0.61), so decorrelating shrinks the region the detector cannot see while weakening it over the region it can. Second falsified prediction in this project.

result 2b (RQ1) — the region the vote cannot see

The vote is blind there. The record is not.

On the 12,181 questions where all four models agreed, disagreement is 0 by construction. Confidence was the only thing this project had looked at, and it reaches AUC 0.592 — real, and useless as a gate. Every other column of the record, combined: 0.772.

8.4%wrong, if you answer everything
6.8%keeping half, on confidence
3.0%keeping half, on the record
2.1%keeping the surest quarter

This is the first direct test of the project's design claim — that keeping the whole record, rather than just the score, is what makes the findings possible.

Out-of-fold, and audited for leakage before it was believed: the first version had a leak (test-fold labels reached a neighbourhood feature used in training), and the first fix was half a fix. A third: duplicate questions sat on both sides of a fold, inflating the earlier published 0.811 until folds were grouped by question text. All three were corrected. It does not rescue the blind region as a class — it ranks within it — and it needs a labelled corpus to place a new question against.

result 7 — is it ignorance, or is it belief?

Tell it that it might be wrong. Almost nothing moves.

Retrieval and search both ask whether the model can find the fact. These two prompts ask whether it holds the belief — no documents, no retriever. Run on all 950 blind spots and on 317 questions the ensemble already had right, because "repaired 1%" means nothing without what it broke.

Counter-evidence does not move the belief either way: requiring a majority of the four to be wrong, it repairs 0.7% and breaks 0.6%. Permission to abstain repairs zero of 950 — upper bound 0.40%.

An earlier version of this slide said counter-evidence breaks four answers for every one it repairs. 10 of its 12 “broken” controls were 2–2 ties, where the tie rule, not the model, decided the verdict; that claim is retired.

And the hedging is real: at least one model abstained on 55.1% of blind spots against 28.4% of correct ones — a 1.9× lift. It hedges more often on what it is about to get wrong, and it never converts.

The behavioural twin of the confidence result, reached from the other side: RQ1 measured a number the model emits, this measured what it does when asked outright. Same answer.

a correction to our own headline

How many of the 950 are real?

A blind spot is a question the automatic grader marked wrong for all four models. So the first thing to ask about 950 is how many of them the grader got wrong. Until 25 August 2026 this project answered “at most 5.6%”. That number counted one kind of mistake and was reported as though it counted all of them.

question setgrader too strict
halueval n=366 33.9%
triviaqa n=140 33.6%
popqa n=439 6.2%
all of them 21.1%

All 950 sent to a judge with the grade hidden. 750 survive as genuine — a rate of 2.2%, not 2.8%.

What the grader called wrong

“Bangladesh” for “People’s Republic of Bangladesh”
“1640s” for “40’s”
“The bumblebee bat” for “Bat”
“Constantine X Doukas” for “Konstantinos X Doukas”
“Smoking” for “Tobacco or alcohol”

The old test looked for answers inside the key. None of these is inside anything, so it could never have found them. A bound is only as wide as the test that produced it.

This makes our headline smaller. It is on a slide because most biases this project has found in itself have run the other way.

result 4b (RQ3) — and it retires one of our own claims

The grader is stricter on the newer models. We had assumed it was not.

Every generation result compares two arms, and only one of them had ever been adjudicated. The gap was covered by an argument: both arms share a grader, so a uniform strictness cancels. It is not uniform. The 2024 arm's 823 blind spots were judged with the same judge, prompt and rule.

judge20242025gap
Qwen3-235B — in the 2025 ensemble 15.19% 21.05% +5.9 pts
Olmo-3.1-32B — in neither arm 17.62% 24.63% +7.0 pts

Two judges, because the first result had to be attacked before it could be believed. The case representative is the first model in sorted order — so every 2025 case was judged on Qwen3-235B's own answer, by Qwen3-235B. Self-favouring inflates exactly this gap. Re-judging both arms with Olmo-3.1-32B, which is in neither ensemble, widened it.

the three categoriespersistedemergedratio
as graded 320 630 1.97×
adjudicated 239 511 2.14×

The ratio survives and slightly strengthens: an upgrade still creates about twice as many newly hidden cases as it inherits. The growth in the total is not significant — 698 genuine blind spots become 750; paired over the same questions, exact McNemar gives p = 0.101 (Olmo-3.1-32B: p = 0.226). That claim is withdrawn.

When a measurement carries a known systematic error, prefer the ratio to the level — the level carries the error, the ratio mostly cancels it.

the part a slide deck usually leaves out

Twice, this project produced a clean result that was not true

Both times the table looked finished. Nothing crashed, no error appeared, and the numbers were internally consistent. Here is what each one actually was, and the check that caught it.

1 · We were not testing the models we thought

The experiment compared 2024 models against 2025 models. We asked the provider for the 2024 model and it quietly served the 2025 one under the old name for some of the models — so the comparison partly measured the new models against themselves. Two whole experiments?, 340,728 answers, measured nothing and were thrown away.

caught by: are these two "different" models giving identical answers? up to 99.6% identical.
fixed by: a gate that checks model identity before any paid run starts

2 · A finding produced by a broken tool

The result read "giving models documents does not help them" — a clean table, reproduced three times. At first the search step returned nothing at all (cached rate-limit errors), so the models were handed blank pages. Once that was fixed, searching on the whole question returned off-topic pages.

caught by: is the correct answer even present in the text we handed over? 8%.
fixed by: fetching the entity's own Wikipedia page instead. 8% → 70%, and the result reversed

78 defects found, 78 fixed, zero open. Every one, its cause and its fix is written down in ISSUES.md; every design choice and the options rejected are in DECISIONS.md.

and this became a finding of its own

Most biases we found in the numbers made our own results look better

Bugs should land in random directions. These did not — most biases found in the numbers leaned the same way, toward the answer we were hoping for. Four examples:

  • Small samples looked better than they were. Every effect measured on 300 questions came out smaller, or vanished, at full scale — five times out of five.
  • The earlier graders were too generous: when they erred, they marked wrong answers correct — which hides blind spots and flatters the detection score. Hand-labelling later cleared the final grader (31 generous vs 39 harsh, across 244 judged cases in the target stratum); the bias was real in the three that came before it.
  • The one bug that tilted the head-to-head comparison handicapped the competing method: its arm ran without retries.
  • Fixing things created things. Three of the four defects found in one audit were introduced by that same day's fixes.

Why this belongs in the thesis

If the biases had been random, about half would have made the results look worse. Few did, and the exceptions are logged. So "the errors were small, and in our favour" is not a sentence anyone should accept — including us.

A pipeline that can produce a confident, coherent, wrong table is the normal case, not the exception. So the methodology chapter is about how each number was attacked, not just what it came out as.
