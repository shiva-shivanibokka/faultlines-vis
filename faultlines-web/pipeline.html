OVERVIEW

The whole pipeline

Fourteen levels, one continuous flow — one dangerous case.

Questions enter at the top. Four models turn each one into four answers. Those answers are tested, stored, and split into a map of where questions live and a signal of who was wrong. The five research questions then probe that record — all built on the one case where the models agree and are wrong.

Why this step existsThe thesis is one sentence: when models from different developers all agree and are all wrong — silent consensus — every disagreement-based detector reads ‘safe’ and is wrong. Following the data end to end shows exactly where the borrowed signal stops and where this project’s work — measuring that blind spot — starts.

Skip it and…you are left asserting novelty in prose, which is the thing the feedback asked you to stop doing.

Use next level → to walk down. Nothing advances on its own. At every level the output on the right becomes the input on the left of the next.

LEVEL 1

Sample the questions

Four datasets in, one frozen corpus out.

in
4 public datasets, by role
proc
load, dedupe, freeze — seed 42
out
34,062 questions, one frozen set

Silent consensus is rare — about 1 question in 36 — but the corpus is not screened down to it. All 34,062 questions are asked and graded, so every rate on this page is measured against the whole set rather than a filtered slice.

Why this step existsFour datasets by role: PopQA supplies obscurity (retrieval should fix it), non-adversarial TruthfulQA supplies shared misconceptions (retrieval should not), and TriviaQA and HaluEval supply the volume and variety that make the rates estimable. SimpleQA was dropped for size — too small to carry a subgroup — and it also ran near the floor (91% of answers wrong for the 7–9B local ensemble, 74.6% hosted). AbstentionBench was designed in as the misconception arm and rejected on ingest: it ships a loading script, unsupported from datasets 4.0. The fixed seed makes the whole study re-runnable.

Skip it and…the persistence test has nothing to compare against, and no one can reproduce the numbers.

Each dataset earns its place by the role it plays in a later question. PopQA is the retrieval positive control (Wikidata-backed, answers guaranteed retrievable); non-adversarial TruthfulQA is the misconception arm, where retrieval is predicted to fail (RQ4).

TruthfulQA is small — 340 questions after filtering — and every number resting on it carries an interval about 6 times wider than TriviaQA's, and about 10 times wider than PopQA's or HaluEval's (AUC 0.683 [95% CI 0.601, 0.764], against 0.861 [95% CI 0.848, 0.874] on TriviaQA). Quote it with the interval attached, never bare.

Deduplicated by content, not by id. 8,489 questions were removed because they were the same question twice: TriviaQA's no-context split lists each question once per evidence source, and some PopQA rows repeat template, subject and answer exactly. Every earlier check worked on question ids, which are unique by construction — unique ids are not unique questions.

LEVEL 2

Ask the four models

One question in, four answers out.

in
one question, identical prompt
proc
4 open-weight models, 4 makers, hosted
out
4 answers + a confidence each

Four different makers is supposed to mean four independent brains with independent mistakes. It does not. Measured on all 34,062 questions and compared within the same dataset, the four are wrong together 3.3× as often as independence predicts — watch which model is answering, then read the red panel.

Why this step existsFour different makers, because two models from the same family share training data and therefore share mistakes. What matters is decorrelated errors, not raw capability: four excellent models that fail on the same questions are weak here, while four ordinary ones that fail differently are exactly what the method needs. This is the assumption the whole disagreement heuristic rests on, and measuring it is the main finding of this project — see the red panel.

Skip it and…you are back to one model checking itself, which is blind to the confident, repeated errors this whole project is about.

Because the models are open-weight, each answer also yields its token log-probabilities. Confidence = exp(mean log p), the inverse of perplexity. Many black-box APIs withhold this; the provider used here exposes it, which is what made the hosted run possible at all.

The diversity risk, measured. The method assumes these four fail differently. They are all web-corpus trained, and the earlier version of this page said the correlation was worth measuring before committing. It was measured, on the full corpus rather than a slice, and the assumption does not hold:

— all four wrong together, within the same dataset: 10,819 questions observed against 3,257 expected under independence — 3.3× (95% CI 3.24–3.41)
— mean pairwise error correlation within each dataset φ = 0.44–0.60 (independence would be 0)

Why the within-dataset figure leads. Pooled over the whole corpus the numbers look larger — all four wrong on 31.8% against 5.2% predicted, 6.1×, φ 0.654 — but that multiplies error rates averaged over datasets whose difficulty differs several-fold, and pooling alone manufactures co-failure: every model is wrong far more often on PopQA than on TriviaQA, so pooled across datasets the four look more correlated than they are within any one. Comparing within a dataset removes that. The pooled figure is kept, and demoted.

Per-model error rates, pooled: Qwen3-235B 44.0% · Gemma-3-27B 52.7% · Llama-4-Maverick 44.1% · Mistral-Small-3.2 51.3%.

Why this is the finding and not just a flaw. Disagreement-based detectors rely on the assumption this measurement refutes. One plausible cause, not tested here, is that all four learned from much of the same web — in which case a differently-trained model would reduce the correlation but not remove it. The 2.8% of questions where the four are not merely jointly wrong but identically wrong is where the heuristic is not weakened but blind.

Quantization caveat. The 2024 comparison arm runs 4-bit, so some of its measured disagreement could be a quantization artifact. The quantization level is identical across all four models in that arm so the noise is uniform, and a q8 replication bounds it: safe for rates, not for membership — about a third of which questions are blind spots changes with precision, so no single question should be read as settled.

LEVEL 3

Grade every answer

Automatic — no human grades correctness.

in
4 answers + the dataset's answer key
proc
4 tests: exact → alias → contains → meaning
out
right / wrong per answer

Cheapest first. Exact string match, then the dataset's alias list, then whether the key is contained in the answer, then a semantic match — cosine similarity between sentence-transformer embeddings. Anything that passes none is graded wrong. TruthfulQA is graded by entailment (NLI) instead.

Why this step existsYou cannot claim “disagreement predicts error” without knowing who was actually wrong. Grading supplies that truth. Note the split: the answer key is used to evaluate the signal, never to compute it.

Skip it and…you have a number that correlates with nothing you can check — an unfalsifiable claim rather than a finding.

Plain string comparison would fail “It’s Canberra, the capital.” The embedding rescues paraphrase — cosine 0.88 → correct. Cosine cannot see negation, so it decides only a small share of answers (2.5% are marked correct by it); its limits are reported in Chapter 4 (§4.4).

LEVEL 4

Score the disagreement

Four answers in, one number out.

in
4 answers + 4 confidences
proc
group by meaning → count → spread
out
one disagreement score, 0 to 0.75

Group the answers by meaning so paraphrase isn't counted as disagreement. The score is 1 minus the largest group's share of the four answers: 0 when all four agree, 0.75 when all four differ. This head-count score is the headline (AUC 0.840). A variant that weights each group by the summed confidence of its members adds almost nothing at this scale.

Why this step existsTwo design choices do the work. Grouping by meaning stops “JFK” and “John F. Kennedy” counting as a fight. Weighting by confidence was tested as a way to stop one hesitant outlier looking like a real split; it is a variant (AUC 0.844), and the headline 0.840 uses the plain head-count score.

Skip it and…the score is mostly noise: paraphrase inflates it, and a single unsure model can fake a serious disagreement.

This score uses no answer key at all — that is what makes the signal label-free. Confidence weighting is the white-box option: it needs token probabilities. It was tested as a variant (0.844 against the head-count 0.840) and is not the headline score.

LEVEL 5

Place it on the map

384 dimensions in, two out — and four uses.

in
question text · score · right-wrong
proc
position from meaning, colour toggled
out
the headline visual test

The map has two inputs, not one. The question wording is embedded and flattened by UMAP to decide where each dot sits. The Level 4 disagreement score is then painted on as colour. Position and colour are computed by completely separate paths.

Why this step existsYes: the map is built from both. Embedding gives every question a position from its meaning; the Level 4 score is then applied as colour. They are kept on separate paths for two reasons. First, non-circularity — if you positioned questions using disagreement, then “high-disagreement questions cluster” would be true by construction and prove nothing; because position comes from wording alone, any colour pattern is a real finding. Second, localization depends on it — a brand-new question can be embedded and placed on the map without running a single model, which is the only reason a new question can be routed before it is answered. On its own, Level 4 gives thousands of separate numbers; joined to position they become regions, and three of the five research questions are claims about regions rather than about individual questions.

Skip it and…you would have scores with no geometry: localization has no neighbourhood to average over, persistence has nothing to overlay, and the retrieval test has no strata to separate. And if you instead laid questions out by their scores, the headline result would be circular — true by construction, worth nothing.

This separation is deliberate. If position were derived from the disagreement score, high-disagreement questions would sit together by construction and the finding would be circular. Because position comes from wording alone — which never sees a model answer — any clustering of colour is a genuine result.

LEVEL 6

Test it against a fair baseline

Same budget. One model, or four?

in
the same questions for both arms
proc
4 generations, allocated two ways
out
two ROC-AUC numbers

The baseline is Semantic Entropy (Kuhn 2023; Farquhar, Nature 2024). It asks one model the same question several times, groups its own answers by meaning, and measures the spread — self-inconsistency. FaultLines asks four different models once each. Same budget of four answers; the main difference is where they come from (the temperatures differ too — see the note).

Why this step existsA number on its own means nothing — AUC 0.840 is only impressive next to something. The baseline is Semantic Entropy, the standard single-model detector: sample one model several times and measure how much it contradicts itself. Running it on the same questions turns “four models beat one” from an assumption into a measurement. The design point is that both arms get the same generation budget — four generations per question either way — so the main thing that differs is whether those four come from one model or four different ones. That isolates model diversity as the main variable. What is matched: the budget of four answers per question, the same sentence-transformer and threshold for grouping answers, and the same answer key. The temperatures differ, by necessity: the ensemble answers at temperature 0, while Semantic Entropy samples at 1.0, because it needs varied samples from one model to measure anything.

Skip it and…you report a lone AUC with nothing to compare it to, and a reviewer can fairly say the same result might come from any single model sampled four times — which is the cheaper, already-published method.

What is matched: four answers per question in both arms, the same sentence-transformer and threshold to group answers, and the same answer key. What is not: temperature. The ensemble answers at 0; the baseline samples at 1.0 (the value the Semantic Entropy papers use), since sampling one model at 0 would return the same answer four times. Result, and it reversed between scales. On the 300-question pilot the best single model's Semantic Entropy BEAT the ensemble. At full scale it does not: cross-model disagreement 0.838 vs the best single model's 0.778 (Mistral-Small-3.2), on the same 6,436 questions at a matched budget of four answers either way (0.840 is the ensemble's AUC on all 34,062). Quote the full-scale pair and never the pilot's — the reversal is itself recorded, in RESULTS claim 2. Detection was never the contribution regardless: the map and the five research questions are things a single-model score cannot produce.

What a high AUC will NOT tell you. (1) Nothing about the all-agree-all-wrong cases — they score exactly zero, are graded errors (about four in five genuine after adjudication), and drag the AUC down, which is exactly why RQ1 measures silent consensus as the hero question; a strong AUC and a large blind spot can coexist. (2) Nothing about where the errors are — one number cannot say “obscure mid-century films”. That is what the map adds, which is why this level is the project’s foundation rather than its result.

LEVEL 7

What the paper stops at

Same signal. Everything after this is the difference.

in
the reproduced Detect result
proc
compare what is produced vs what is kept
out
the gap FaultLines fills

Hamidieh et al. (ICLR 2026) and Gorbett & Jana (2026) produce one number per question and a calibration curve — then stop. Producing that number required a rich record, and they discard it.

Why this step existsThis is the level that answers the feedback directly. The signal is the same; what happens to it is not. Putting the two side by side makes the contribution concrete instead of rhetorical.

Skip it and…the honest answer to “isn't this already done?” has to be improvised, which is exactly where the last review went badly.

This level is the argument for the whole project: same signal, different ending. Everything to the right of the divider is what the next seven levels (8–14) build.

LEVEL 8

Store the record

Kept, indexed, and split two ways.

in
the full record for every question
proc
written to columnar parquet on disk
out
a MAP branch and a SIGNAL branch

The record is persisted, not held in memory: four answer texts, four grades, four confidences, the embedding, the map position and the entity metadata. Then it splits, and every finding downstream reads one branch or the other.

Why this step existsPrior work computes the score and drops the rest. Keeping the record is the contribution — every finding downstream is only possible because the answers, grades, confidences and coordinates still exist. Because the vectors are kept too, “questions near this fault” is a nearest-neighbour query over the stored array rather than a re-run.

Skip it and…every finding would need the four models re-run from scratch — hours of GPU time per question you already answered.

The 384-d question vectors live in question_vectors.parquet, so “find questions semantically near this fault line” is a scikit-learn NearestNeighbors call over an array already in memory (see rq2_localize.py). No database and no vector service run anywhere in this project. A Postgres + pgvector tier was in the original design and was dropped once the corpus turned out to fit comfortably in files — the whole record table is a few megabytes. Keeping it would have added an operational dependency that can be down on the day of an evaluation, and bought nothing.

LEVEL 9

Check the grader by hand

The human step — small, bounded, and blinded.

in
graded TruthfulQA answers — 2 blinded strata
proc
one verdict per answer, machine grade hidden
out
430 verdicts + a grader error rate

The only place a human touches the data. A blinded command-line pass shows the question, one model's answer and the accepted gold answers — never the machine's verdict — and asks one question: correct, wrong, or cannot tell? 430 verdicts on TruthfulQA answers, across two strata the labeller cannot distinguish.

Why this step exists“Label-free” applies to the signal, not to the analysis. Whether an answer is genuinely wrong or merely worded differently from the answer key is a judgement no metric makes, and it decides whether silent consensus is a real phenomenon or a grading artifact. These 430 verdicts — all on TruthfulQA answers, where the automatic grader is weakest — were the first audit of the grader. The artifact rate itself is now measured rather than argued about: a blind adjudication pass over the 950 blind spots (950 decided) finds 21.1% of them are grading artifacts (95% CI 18.6–23.8%).

Skip it and…RQ1 cannot separate genuine silent consensus from grading noise, and the headline number rests on trusting an automatic grader that has never been audited.

What this is not. An earlier design had these labels training a cause classifier that would then tag the remaining thousands automatically, and splitting errors into obscurity / staleness / misconception. No classifier was ever built, and the RQ4 cause strata are rule-based instead (rarest PopQA entities for obscurity, TruthfulQA errors for misconception) — which is more auditable and needs no training data. This panel used to describe the classifier as though it existed; it did not.

Two things this panel used to call open are now measured. The hand labels were first compared with the machine grader. Agreement between humans was measured separately: a second annotator labelled 115 cases and agrees with the first at Cohen's κ = 0.970. And the grading-artifact rate is no longer the one-annotator bound this level once reported; it comes from a blind adjudication pass: 21.1% of the blind spots.

RQ1 · MEASURE

Measure silent consensus

When all four agree — and all four are wrong.

in
the Level 4 score × the Level 3 grade
proc
isolate all-agree-all-wrong; read confidence
out
the blind spot, named and quantified

Agreement is the Level 4 score; correctness is the Level 3 grade. Crossing them exposes the blind spot: all four agree AND all four are wrong, so the vote fires nothing. The claim: ensemble confidence still separates right from wrong there — the full run measures AUC 0.592 across all 12,181 agreement-region questions, where vote-counting is 0.5 by construction.

Why this step existsThis is the hero question. Every disagreement detector reads silent consensus as safe and is wrong. Prior work on correlated errors measures the phenomenon, but not this region for a disagreement detector on factual questions; doing so — and showing a white-box confidence signal survives where the black-box vote dies — is the contribution.

Skip it and…the project presents a detector while staying silent about the one error class it structurally cannot see.

Kim et al. (2025), ‘Correlated errors in large language models’, shows models agree on wrong answers more than chance, so this does not claim to discover the phenomenon — it names, locates and quantifies it, and adds the confidence signal a black-box method cannot have.

RQ2 · LOCALIZE

Localize the blind spots

Do they cluster — beyond just obscurity?

in
question wording + entity popularity
proc
map density, controlled for popularity
out
characterizable error regions

Do the blind spots scatter at random, or concentrate? Position comes from wording alone, so clustering of error is genuine. And it must survive a popularity control — it does: on 13,760 PopQA questions, popularity correlates with error at -0.287 while neighbourhood density correlates at +0.303, and density adds to the AUC on top of popularity. Two densities are reported and they are not interchangeable: +0.277 over the shared map — what this tool draws, and what a user brushing a region actually sees — and +0.303 recomputed within PopQA, which is what the control requires, because roughly a quarter of a PopQA question’s fifteen map-neighbours come from a different dataset.

Why this step existsIf shared errors just tracked obscurity, this would be a known 2023 result (Kandpal; Mallen). The claim is stronger: blind spots cluster in characterizable regions and carry signal beyond popularity. Measured on the full corpus: popularity correlates with error at -0.287 and neighbourhood density at +0.303 (PopQA, n=13,760) — and controlling for popularity, density still adds to the AUC.

Skip it and…‘errors concentrate somewhere’ is not a finding unless the regions are characterizable and the clustering beats both chance and popularity.

Position from wording is what makes this non-circular and lets an unanswered question be placed on the map — which is what RQ4 needs to route a new question before paying for it.

RQ3 · PERSIST

Test whether blind spots persist

Plate tectonics — do the fault lines move?

in
the same frozen questions, twice
proc
run an older ensemble alongside
out
the persistent core

Everything so far is a snapshot of today's models. Persistence asks whether the cracks move: as models upgrade, do the same silent-consensus regions survive, or heal? A persistent core would suggest something the model generations share, such as their training data.

Why this step existsA blind spot that survives a whole model generation is more likely a structural limit (possibly inherited from shared training data, not tested here) than a bug awaiting the next release. That is the durability answer — the reason this stays relevant past any single model — and it is only testable because Level 1 froze the sample.

Skip it and…every result is a snapshot of today's models, with no way to tell a permanent blind spot from one the next release already fixed.

The non-adversarial filter applies to TruthfulQA only — its adversarial half was written against GPT-3, which would confound a cross-generation test. The other three datasets run in full, so both arms cover the same 34,062 questions.

What the comparison found. The newer ensemble is more accurate, and its blind spots do not shrink. Overall error falls 53.5% —> 41.1%. The grader counts 823 —> 950 blind spots, but the genuine ones, adjudicated case by case, go 698 —> 750: not a significant change (exact McNemar p = 0.101; outside judge 678 —> 716, p = 0.226). What does rise is the share of the remaining errors that are shared: 4.5% —> 6.8%, 1.50× (95% CI 1.39–1.62). One generation pair, with size and generation changing together.

The confound, stated plainly. The two arms differ in three ways, not one: generation (2024 vs 2025), scale (7–9B dense vs 17–27B active, two of them MoE), and serving (local 4-bit vs hosted). Only precision is partially controlled, by a q8 replication on a sample of the corpus. So this level measures what happens when a deployed ensemble is upgraded — which in practice always means newer AND larger — and it cannot separate generation from scale. Isolating them needs a 2024-generation arm at 24–27B on the same provider; that arm is specified and costed but has not been run, and no fully size-matched four-maker set exists (Meta's 2025-generation Llama models (Llama 4) are all 17B active parameters or larger).

RQ4 · RETRIEVAL

Test the retrieval dissociation

Retrieval fixes some errors — not shared misconceptions.

in
errors, split by cause
proc
re-answer closed-book vs with retrieval
out
what retrieval fixes — and what it can't

Split the errors by cause and give each retrieval. Obscurity (never in the weights) should be fixed — a document supplies the fact. Shared misconceptions should not — retrieval may draw on the same corpus that produced the myth. The predicted dissociation is the finding.

Why this step existsThe most practically useful claim: it tells anyone building a RAG system which errors their retrieval layer will not save them from. The measured result: documents fix +65 points of obscurity errors but only +14 of misconceptions, and that +14 is not statistically significant (p = 0.077); the gap between them, +51.2 points, is the claim. One untested explanation is that retrieval draws from the same web that taught the misconception. An experiment that predicts a dissociation — helps here, not there — is far stronger than ‘retrieval improves accuracy’, and if it's wrong that is still a publishable result.

Skip it and…you would be left asserting that retrieval can't fix misconceptions in prose — the exact move the feedback said to stop making. Measure it instead.

The corpus is itself a variable: a curated source (Wikipedia debunks common myths) versus open web. The sharpened claim: retrieval corrects an error only when the corpus holds an explicit correction — and for misconceptions the myth may be more retrievable than its correction. That explanation is untested.

RQ5 — agentic web search, reported here. Letting the models search the web for themselves does not rescue the blind spots either: 64.7% of them (88 of 136) stay wrong (95% CI 56.4–72.2%). It has no level of its own because it reuses this set-up, with a search tool in place of fixed documents.

RISK REGISTER

What could go wrong

Nine risks — three answered by the full run, three open for your input.

in
every risk worth naming
proc
answered by the full run, or left open on purpose
out
3 answered · 3 decisions · 3 carried

The upstream risk was always that the four models fail together — if they do, every finding downstream loses its foundation. It was measured first, not last — and the answer is yes, they do, on 950 questions where all four agreed and all four were wrong. The signal still lands at AUC 0.840, in the same range as the published work it builds on (measured on different questions), because those 950 are 7.8% of the agreement region rather than most of it. The risk was not retired; it became the thesis.

Why this step existsA proposal that only lists risks asks the reader to trust that they are survivable. Three of these now have answers from the full run — 34,062 questions, 136,248 graded answers — so the biggest unknowns are numbers rather than hopes. One did not come back the way the proposal hoped, and it is stated here as it landed. The three in the middle column are genuine forks where a supervisor's judgement changes the design.

Skip it and…the first hard question in the defence is one I have already answered but never wrote down, and the open decisions get made alone and late.

The middle column is what I would most like your input on. Each is a fork where the data is in but the call is a design judgement, not a calculation.

Measured on the full run — 34,062 questions, 136,248 graded answers, zero failed calls. 78 defects were found and fixed along the way; every one, its cause and its fix is in ISSUES.md.

LEVEL 14

Everything lands in the tool

Five linked views.

in
one exported data.json — records + labels
proc
a single static page, canvas 2D
out
five coordinated views

The tool is not a wrapper on a number. Each view supports one of the analysis tasks, and a labelling panel lets reviewers mark cases and export their verdicts. (The 430 blinded hand labels in the thesis were made with a separate command-line script.)

Why this step existsThe tool is not a write-up of the findings — it produces them. Each view supports one task, and the labelling panel lets a reviewer mark cases and export their verdicts. That is also what makes this a visual-analytics thesis rather than an ML report

Skip it and…the labelling has nowhere to happen, and the findings become static figures a reader has to take on trust.

One static page, data baked in, no server. An earlier design had three tiers — Neon Postgres → FastAPI on Hugging Face Spaces → React on Vercel — and it was dropped on purpose. The evaluation was designed around participants opening a link, and a server was one more thing that could be down on the day. Everything the tool needs ships in the page and one JSON file; state, brushing and linked selection are plain JavaScript; the map is a 2D canvas and the ensemble charts are D3.
